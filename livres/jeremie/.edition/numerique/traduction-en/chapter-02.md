# Before I formed thee: the call {#appel}

::: epigraphe
> Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations.
>
> (Jeremiah 1:5)
:::

The son of Hilkiah was living among the priests of Anathoth when it all began for him. It was the thirteenth year of King Josiah, around 627 BC by the usual reckoning. The book gives neither the place nor the hour of what happened that day. It reports the words, and those words carried Jeremiah to the end of his life.

## A word that comes

The first three verses of the book speak of Jeremiah in the third person. At the fourth, the tone changes: "Then the word of the Lord came unto me, saying" (Jeremiah 1:4). The prophet himself takes up the story of his call. He tells it plainly, without scenery.

Moses had seen a bush that "burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed" (Exodus 3:2). Isaiah had seen "the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up" (Isaiah 6:1). Jeremiah's account describes nothing of the kind. There is a word, then a dialogue. God speaks, Jeremiah answers, God speaks again. The rest of the book resembles this first exchange: a man receives a word he did not choose, and he must pass it on to people who do not want to hear it.

Here is the scene, as Jeremiah reports it:

> Then the word of the Lord came unto me, saying, Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations. Then said I, Ah, Lord God! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child. But the Lord said unto me, Say not, I am a child: for thou shalt go to all that I shall send thee, and whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak. Be not afraid of their faces: for I am with thee to deliver thee, saith the Lord.
>
> (Jeremiah 1:4-8)

In five verses everything is laid down: what God has done, what Jeremiah fears, what God commands and what he promises. We will take them up one by one.

## Known before birth

Before telling Jeremiah what he must do, God tells him what he has already done for him. The verbs are in the past: "I knew thee", "I sanctified thee", "I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations" (Jeremiah 1:5). When God first speaks to him, Jeremiah has said nothing and accomplished nothing. Yet the text places his mission before his birth, before God even "formed thee in the belly".

This word overturns the order we are used to. Usually a task is entrusted to someone who has proved himself. Here God's knowledge comes first, then the consecration, then the sending. Jeremiah's qualities do not enter into the account. He discovers that he was expected before he could prepare himself.

The apostle Paul would speak of his own call in similar words. He mentions God, "who separated me from my mother's womb, and called me by his grace" (Galatians 1:15). Like Jeremiah, he understands his life in the light of a decision of God that came before him.

Many readers wonder what this verse says beyond Jeremiah. Some read in it a word about every human life before birth. Others see in it the election of every believer, or else the particular call of a prophet. The text itself is addressed to one particular man and speaks of his mission. This book keeps to what the passage says, and leaves each reader to explore these questions further with the teaching of his or her church.

The title God gives Jeremiah is also surprising: "a prophet unto the nations". The young man comes from a town of priests in the land of Benjamin (Jeremiah 1:1). Yet his word will reach far beyond Judah. A large part of his book is addressed to Egypt, to the Philistines, to Moab, to Edom, to Babylon and to other peoples, under this heading: "The word of the Lord which came to Jeremiah the prophet against the Gentiles" (Jeremiah 46:1). The horizon of his mission reached far beyond his home town.

::: encadre
**Historical background: the thirteenth year of Josiah**

The book dates Jeremiah's call precisely: "in the thirteenth year of his reign", that is, the reign of Josiah (Jeremiah 1:2). Josiah had become king at eight and reigned thirty-one years in Jerusalem (2 Kings 22:1). So he was about twenty when Jeremiah was called, and he had eighteen years of reign left. Historians usually place this thirteenth year around 627 BC.

Another passage confirms this starting point. In the fourth year of King Jehoiakim, Jeremiah says to the people: "From the thirteenth year of Josiah the son of Amon king of Judah, even unto this day, that is the three and twentieth year, the word of the Lord hath come unto me" (Jeremiah 25:3).

Jeremiah's age, on the other hand, is given nowhere. The word "child" that he uses (Jeremiah 1:6) shows how he saw himself before the task; it does not allow us to work out his age. Nor does the text say where he was when the word came to him.
:::

## "I cannot speak"

Jeremiah's answer fits in one sentence: "Ah, Lord God! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child" (Jeremiah 1:6). He gives two reasons. He cannot speak, and he is too young. Both objections touch what is most visible in a prophet: his mouth and his authority.

Jeremiah is not the first to draw back in this way. Centuries earlier, Moses had answered God: "O my Lord, I am not eloquent" (Exodus 4:10). Isaiah, before the holiness of God, had cried out: "Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5). For these three men, the difficulty lies in the same place, the mouth. They know that a prophet speaks in the name of another, and they measure the distance between what they are and what is asked of them.

It would be easy to read Jeremiah's answer as a lack of faith. The text does not condemn it. God does not rebuke him for being afraid; he only forbids him to stop at that fear. Jeremiah's weakness is real, and God does not pretend otherwise. He never tells him: "You speak well" or "You are old enough". He moves the question elsewhere.

## A command and a promise

"Say not, I am a child" (Jeremiah 1:7). God's answer is a command. It does not argue about age or talent. It turns Jeremiah's eyes towards the one who sends him: "for thou shalt go to all that I shall send thee, and whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak" (Jeremiah 1:7).

Twice the verse leaves no exception: "all that I shall send thee", "whatsoever I command thee". Jeremiah will not choose his audience, nor the words he will carry. He will go to those God points out, kings or peasants, priests or soldiers. He will say what God gives him, even when that message makes him hated. His freedom is limited by obedience, but so is his responsibility: he will not have to invent the message, nor to make it pleasant.

Then comes the promise: "Be not afraid of their faces: for I am with thee to deliver thee" (Jeremiah 1:8). This promise contains a warning. Only someone in danger needs to be delivered. God is telling Jeremiah that he will have reasons to be afraid. What he promises is his presence in the midst of the threats.

The book will show how much this promise was needed. Many years later, Jeremiah would be let down with cords into a dungeon, where he would sink in the mire (Jeremiah 38:6). The young man from Anathoth did not know it yet, but the word received that day was already waiting for him there.

## Words in the mouth

The gesture that follows answers the objection exactly. "Then the Lord put forth his hand, and touched my mouth. And the Lord said unto me, Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth" (Jeremiah 1:9). Jeremiah had said he could not speak. God touches that mouth and places his own words in it.

The comparison with Isaiah is striking. For him, a seraph had taken a live coal from off the altar, had touched his lips and had told him that his sin was purged (Isaiah 6:6-7). For Jeremiah, the Lord stretches out his own hand. Here the text speaks of words given, with no mention of sin or purification. Jeremiah's problem was inability, and God gives him what he lacks.

Then comes the mission, summed up in a single verse: "See, I have this day set thee over the nations and over the kingdoms, to root out, and to pull down, and to destroy, and to throw down, to build, and to plant" (Jeremiah 1:10).

Six verbs, in three pairs. The first four speak of tearing down, the last two of building. The proportion sets the tone of Jeremiah's ministry: many of his messages will be announcements of judgment. Yet the sentence does not stop at ruin. It ends with "build" and "plant". Judgment prepares a rebuilding.

The prophet will receive neither an army nor political power. He will root out and plant by the word. These same verbs will come back several times in the book, and God will take them up for himself when he promises the return of his people: "so will I watch over them, to build, and to plant" (Jeremiah 31:28).

## The almond tree and the seething pot

The call goes on with two visions, introduced by the same question. "Jeremiah, what seest thou?" The young man answers simply: "I see a rod of an almond tree" (Jeremiah 1:11). And God explains: "Thou hast well seen: for I will hasten my word to perform it" (Jeremiah 1:12).

In our translation, the link between the tree and God's word is not explained. The text keeps the lesson God draws from the vision: the word he entrusts to Jeremiah will not be left hanging. God himself watches over it and will bring it to fulfilment.

This assurance protected the prophet from an impossible burden. Jeremiah had to speak the word; he did not have to make it happen. He would need this, for his opponents would mock him by repeating: "Where is the word of the Lord? let it come now" (Jeremiah 17:15). Years would pass between the announcement and its fulfilment. Meanwhile the almond tree is a reminder that God does not sleep.

The second vision is darker. "I see a seething pot; and the face thereof is toward the north" (Jeremiah 1:13). God explains: "Out of the north an evil shall break forth upon all the inhabitants of the land" (Jeremiah 1:14). Peoples will come and set their thrones at the entering of the gates of Jerusalem and against all the cities of Judah (Jeremiah 1:15).

The reason for this judgment is given plainly: the people "have forsaken me, and have burned incense unto other gods, and worshipped the works of their own hands" (Jeremiah 1:16). The disaster to come answers the people's forsaking of their God.

On the day of the call, the enemy has no name. Only in the fourth year of Jehoiakim will Jeremiah identify him clearly: God will send for "Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, my servant" (Jeremiah 25:9). The young prophet first receives the announcement, and clarity will come later.

## A defenced city

The call ends with marching orders:

> Thou therefore gird up thy loins, and arise, and speak unto them all that I command thee: be not dismayed at their faces, lest I confound thee before them. For, behold, I have made thee this day a defenced city, and an iron pillar, and brasen walls against the whole land, against the kings of Judah, against the princes thereof, against the priests thereof, and against the people of the land. And they shall fight against thee; but they shall not prevail against thee; for I am with thee, saith the Lord, to deliver thee.
>
> (Jeremiah 1:17-19)

"Gird up thy loins": the expression comes back elsewhere in Scripture for a man getting ready to act. So Elijah, before setting off, "girded up his loins, and ran before Ahab" (1 Kings 18:46). Jeremiah must be ready, like someone setting out on a journey.

The warning that follows is severe: "be not dismayed at their faces, lest I confound thee before them" (Jeremiah 1:17). The prophet must choose between two fears. If he gives in to the fear of men, he will have more to fear from God. The fear of God, for its part, will set him free from the fear of men.

The list of opponents covers the whole land: the kings, the princes, the priests and the people. The priests are on the list, and Jeremiah is himself a priest's son. He will have to speak against his own circle. The previous chapter showed it: the men of his town and his own brothers will turn against him (Jeremiah 11:21; 12:6).

The promise closes the whole: "And they shall fight against thee; but they shall not prevail against thee" (Jeremiah 1:19). War is announced, and it will come. Pashur will strike him and put him in the stocks (Jeremiah 20:2). King Jehoiakim will burn the scroll of his prophecies (Jeremiah 36:23). Princes will cast him into the dungeon (Jeremiah 38:6). But when Jerusalem is taken, the king of Babylon himself will give this order concerning the prophet: "Take him, and look well to him, and do him no harm" (Jeremiah 39:12). They will fight against him; they will not prevail.

## Twenty-three years later

The account of the call stops at verse 19. The book does not say how Jeremiah left that moment or what he did the next day. But it gives a glimpse of what followed, further on, from the prophet's own mouth.

In the fourth year of Jehoiakim, Jeremiah reminded the people that he had not stopped speaking: "that is the three and twentieth year, the word of the Lord hath come unto me, and I have spoken unto you, rising early and speaking; but ye have not hearkened" (Jeremiah 25:3). Twenty-three years of faithfulness, and almost no visible fruit. The young man who said he could not speak had spoken, again and again.

Those years were not peaceful. Jeremiah knew the joy of the word received: "Thy words were found, and I did eat them" (Jeremiah 15:16). He also knew the temptation to keep silent, and the impossibility of doing so: "his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones" (Jeremiah 20:9). We will meet these two cries again in chapter 5. They show that the word put in his mouth on the day of the call had become part of himself.

Before following Jeremiah into his first years of ministry, under Josiah, let us pause on what his call can say to those who serve God today.

::: lecons
The call comes before competence. God knew, sanctified and ordained Jeremiah before he had done anything (Jeremiah 1:5). When Jeremiah objects that he cannot speak, God answers him with a command and a promise (Jeremiah 1:7-8), without a word about his abilities. Perhaps you have been given a task in your church that seems too big for you: teaching a children's class or leading a time of prayer. Your weakness may be real. God does not ask you to deny it. He asks you not to stop there, and to look to the one who sends you. What he requires, he gives, as he put his words in Jeremiah's mouth (Jeremiah 1:9).

The servant does not choose his message. "Whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak" (Jeremiah 1:7). Jeremiah had to announce many things he would never have chosen to say. We too are tempted to keep the passages we like and leave the others aside. Picture a Sunday school teacher preparing the lesson on Noah. He would like to speak only of the ark and the animals, and pass quickly over the judgment. He can speak of it in words children can understand, without frightening them, and stay faithful to what the text says. What holds for a children's class holds for a pulpit. This obedience is also a release: you do not have to invent the message, only to pass it on.

God's presence does not cancel opposition. "And they shall fight against thee; but they shall not prevail against thee" (Jeremiah 1:19). God did not promise Jeremiah that he would be loved, nor that his road would be easy. He promised to be with him. If you meet resistance while serving God, it does not mean you have taken the wrong road. Jeremiah was beaten and cast into a dungeon, and God kept his word. Do not measure God's faithfulness by the absence of difficulties. Look rather at how he has kept you in the midst of them.

Finally, God watches over his word (Jeremiah 1:12). Whoever teaches, preaches or bears witness often carries a weight that is not theirs: they would like to see the fruit at once. Jeremiah spoke for twenty-three years without being heard (Jeremiah 25:3). Your part is to sow faithfully. The fulfilment belongs to God.
:::
